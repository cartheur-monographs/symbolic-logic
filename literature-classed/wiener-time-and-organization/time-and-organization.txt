The Fawley Foundation was established in the Univer-

sity of Southampton in 1953, by Esso Petroleum Company,

Ltd. In the Deed of Establishment the University and the
Founders expressed their conviction that “‘the future of this
nation and of mankind is closely linked with the relationship between science and industry, and that a special duty
lies upon those engaged in academic and industrial life to
do all in their power to enhance understanding of the major
problems involved.”

The Foundation providesfor the delivery in the University

of a series of annual public lectures under the general title
of “Science and Industry”; and each lecture will treat

important aspects of the application of scientific thought
to industrial and social needs.

The second Fawley Lecture was delivered by Professor
Norbert Wiener, on June 2nd, 1955, in the Assembly
Hall of the University, the Vice-Chancellor presiding.

[® is a great honour and a great pleasure for me to be asked
to give the second Fawley Foundation Lecture. In the first
place, the general scope of the lecture places it among the most
important foundation lectures of modern times. In the second
place, it is in a field in which, while activity has been shared
by Britain, America and other countries, there has been, in
Great Britain, the longest and most continuous social consciousness of the relation between science and industry. For
this reason I feel particularly honoured as an American to give

a lecture in a field in which I am ready to concede a large

share of the social forethought to England. Finally, Sir Harold

lecture. I feel grateful to be his first successor in what is, after

all, to some measure a return engagement in scientific solidarity
between the two countries.

Probably the reason why I have been asked to give this
lecture is that I have written Cybernetics. Cybernetics is the
science which I define to be the science of control and com-

munication both in the machine and in the living organism.
The most conspicuous new aspect of the science of cybernetics
is that of the computing and control machines which work
more or less for the purposes of computation or for the pro-

gramming of automatic factories, and are known according to
the fashion of the day as “giant brains”. They have caught
the public imagination, which is always more receptive to
gadgets than to ideas, and they have become in the eyes of
that considerable section of the public which is addicted to
science fiction, a symbol of the replacement of man by a
superior machine. This was not at all my intention in writing
Cybernetics; and it is not the purpose of my present talk to
contribute to the further self-abnegation of humanity nor to

promote the cult of the gadget.

On the contrary, it is my purpose to explain a certain contribution which cybernetics may have made to the ideas of
communication and of control, whether the ideas are embodied
in the organism, the machine, or unmechanical forms of human
society. Certain researches, of which I want to say something
further on in this lecture, have forced me to consider the

possibility that control and communication mechanisms may
contain clocks, which are essential, if not to their very existence,
at least to their effective performance. The present paper is
devoted to the comparative study of these clocks and of the

function they perform over the entire range of cybernetic ideas.
In connection with the broad range of cybernetic ideas,

[ wish to underline the fact that automatization is merely one

aspect of cybernetics, and that automatization itself will suffer
if it is not taken in a broader context of cybernetic thought.
The notion that communication and control are connected,
and that they represent measurable quantities with a theory
which is a branch of probability theory, has an importance
which is entirely independent of the particular devices or the
particular organism in which the theory is embodied. It is not
to be progressive to insist in and out of season on the automatic
features of our new communication and control machines.

The automatic machine has great advantages for performing
with the utmost speed communication and control operations
of limited complexity; but it does not begin to compete with
the human or even the animal nervous system when the

demands for flexibility and complexity are high. A good

understanding of cybernetics is necessary not only to enable us
to design automatic control machines, but, even more, to
determine whether the problem with which we are dealing is
an appropriate problem for the automatic machine at all. The

proper place for the modern genie, the slave of the electronic

laboratory, is as our slave and not as our master.

and devoted to impressing on peaceful stay-at-home persons
like myself the titillations of vicarious violence. At its best, the
detective story may be a genuine intellectual pursuit, in which
the author shows how small bits of stray information left

and apparently insignificant clues which build up to a com-

To go into the history of the electroencephalogram: some
time in the 1930’s a group of electrophysiologists in various
countries! made a study of the electric potentials collected by
electrodes placed on a human scalp, and of the similar electric

potentials yielded by electrodes placed directly on or in the

brains of experimental animals. Great hopes were formed that
these brain waves would exhibit important characteristics,
throwing a light on mental and cerebral activity in such a

manner that they might be used both by the physiologist in his
investigation of normal activity and by the pathologist in his
study of disease. In fact, these highly irregular curves have been

of considerable use in the study of epilepsy and of a few other

cerebral phenomena. However, the great expectations originally formed from them have been largely disappointed, owing
to the difficulty of getting readable information by the inspec-

was a new method for the analysis and classification of electroencephalograph records. This I found mvself in a position to

furnish.

While the autocorrelation method is fairly recent under its
own name, something very nearly equivalent to it has been

used by the physicists for some time in the study of light. I am
referring to the reading of the interferometer used by Michel-

son. In this instrument, which is the most refined of all

spectrographs, a beam of light is spread by a half-silvered
mirror, and the two halves follow paths with an adjustable
difference in length. They are now allowed to interfere, and
the intensity of the light is studied as a quantity dependent on

the difference in paths. This intensity depends quadratically

on the electromagnetic vector of the light. The places where the
light is strong after interference are known as fringes. The

degree of homogeneity and monochromaticity of the light is

measured by the counted number of the fringes, before they
disappear when the relative difference of delay in the two paths
is great.
Using these criteria we may say that the alpha rhythm in
the brain contains at its centre a rhythm which continues to

show itself as the passage of hundredsoffringesand which
is therefore of extraordinary purity in frequency. This central
rhythm, for all its persistency, is not strong in energy, and
would be entirely lost on any non-instrumental examination

of a brain wave. In this sense, the pure central rhythm is a new

discovery, and the old alpha rhythm as observed is derived
from nothing more than the beginning and exponentially
falling part of the autocorrelation diagram, where the fringes

are strong, but have not had the chance to show their more

important property of consistency.

of the detective story, and I have shown how certain scientific
detective methods have established the existence of an accurate

at about 10 oscillations per second, and keeping phase with

itself for perhaps hundreds of seconds. However, again in the
detective tradition, it is not enough to discover a crime or an

anomaly, but its significance must be established and in many

Well and good. Is there any reason why it should have a good

clock or why a communication and control apparatus using a
good clock can be expected to function better or at least
differently from one with a poor clock? I shall show that the

function of the clock in the control apparatus is important and
that it is quite concordant with an expectation that clocking
in the brain has something to do with intelligence and organized

functioning.

A control and communication mechanism is one in which
stored traces of past decisions are combined to give rise to

present and future decisions. For the combination of informa-

tion, it is generally desirable that the pieces of information
combined should arrive at substantially the same time into the

combining mechanism. This is true with most switching
mechanisms, and it is definitely true with that particular sort
of switching mechanism known as the synapse of the nervous

system.

The transmission of an impulse along a nerve fibre is a

fairly simple phenomenon, if we confine ourselves to its

description rather than its analysis. The electric discharge
known as a nerve spike conditions a similar discharge further

along the fibre proceeding with a constant velocity and nearly
independent of the nature of the stimulus by which it is caused.

In other words, the conduction of nerve impulses is an all-ornone phenomenon.

However, impulses may proceed beyond the fibre on which
they originate. When the fibre ends, it joins with a number of

similar fibres in a butting on a new fibre by connections known

as synapses. When the proper combination of incoming fibres
fire within a certain limited time of combination, the outgoing
fibre fires, and otherwise it does not. That is, the question of
whether an outgoing fibre fires or not depends not merely on

the existence of impulses on the connected incoming fibres but
also on their substantial simultaneity of arrival.
We now say that it is possible for a switching mechanism to
operate without a clock, by operating on what we may call
a sequential basis. If there is a mechanism for holding incoming
impulses until the proper set has arrived and then combining

them simultaneously into outgoing impulses, a controlled

system may work without a clock. This involves a complicated

storage and triggering mechanism, and it also involves the
requirement that the outgoing channels be kept ready and idle

until the proper combination of impulses has passed into the

incoming channels.

On the one hand, such an idleness is time consuming. By
far the greater part of the activity of a control mechanism

operated on this principle will be spent in standing by. On the
other hand, holding mechanisms on temporary memories,
although they exist in the brain as well as in other control
mechanisms, are not too well understood, and are likely to be

complicated and expensive in character. Of course, the word

‘expensive’ is not one to be interpreted in terms of money.
It is certainly not so to be interpreted in the case of a living
system such as the human being; but it does have a meaning

as far as demands on available resources are concerned. Thus

a system which has a good enough clock to ensure that the
impulses which are to combine arise simultaneously, has a

great advantage in economy and in efficiency.

That this is more than a manner of speaking is attested by

the fact that many working control mechanisms do in fact
contain clocks. At the Massachusetts Institute of Technology,
we have a milling machine which reads its orders off punched
tape like the tape in a player-piano, and interprets these into

an up-and-down movement of the work and a right-and-left
and to-and-fro movement of the cutting tool. Some sort of

clock is essential for seeing that corresponding impulses arrive

at corresponding times, and that the three different sorts of
motion remain in step with one another.
It is of course possible that this regulating clock is a clock
only in the Pickwickian sense, and that it merely counts
impulses rather than measures the time between them. In fact,
the clock of our milling machine has something of this character. Its speed can be changed considerably without a great
effect on its efficiency, and can even be changed in an irregular
manner. This possibility, however, depends on certain rather

special and limited features of the milling machine.

The milling machine is deliberately made self-compensating

under a change in clocking speed. What facilitates this is that

most of the cuts are long straight lines. Thus over a large part

of its action, a change in speed merely moves the instrument

further in the direction in which it is going, and the errors

hand, if the milling machine is working on sharply curved cuts

at a high speed, then the inertia of its motion will mean that a

change of the timing will carry the tool and its work in a

more varied work than the milling machine will have a more

cogent reason for keeping its clocking more perfectly timed.

Suppose that we were to examine electric stray currents

around the milling machine, what should we find? On the one

hand, a clock itself would give a very nearly periodic record,

albeit a weak one. Clocks are best kept at a moderate energy

level without too much coupling with the external world. This
is for the sake of their uniformity of operation. We should
expect that the autocorrelation of the clock itself would not be
too dissimilar to that part of the autocorrelation of an electroencephalogram which runs at an approximately constant level,

after the initial period of rapid exponential decline has been

passed.

However, a clock in a machine is there to be used; and it
cannot be used unless its output is amplified up to a level at
which it is effective in accomplishing some mechanical task.
Therefore, from time to time, in addition to stray currents in

the machine which arise from the unamplified clock, there will

be strays whose source is the clock as temporarily amplified for
use. These amplifications will take place over different times,
and they essentially represent modulation of the clock as a

carrier by a message indicating its employment.
This modulated carrier is a phenomenon essentially similar
to what we find in television, where the clock represents the

sweep frequency. This sweep frequency carries the message,

which in the case of television is the picture. The effect of
modulating a carrier wave is to spread the frequency band of
a carrier; and in certain well-known cases this will have the
effect on the autocorrelation of an exponential falling off. Thus

the autocorrelation which we obtain from the brain wave bears
a close resemblance to that which we should expect from the
combination of the readings of a modulated clock and an un-

modulated clock.

From the control machine of the automatic factory to the

lines both have and need clocks. The personnel of a factory
comes in on regular shifts and punches the time-clocks. This is

Moreover, the material of the assembly line must also be
delivered on a tight schedule. Suppose that we consider the

point in a motor car assembly line, where the engine assembly

is clamped to place in the body assembly. Unless engines and
bodies are delivered at the same rate, one or the other line will

machine like the brain, the sequential operation of an organism, as opposed to the clocked organization, makes storage
demands which are likely to be unwelcome and expensive.
As history goes, the factory is rather a new form of social

organization. However, long before human labour in the
factory was clocked by the hour and the minute, large sectors
of human effort were clocked by the day, the year, the month,

and the week. Every civilization, which is worthy of being
called a civilization at all, has a calendar. Here I include such

diverse cultures as the Egyptian, the Sumerian Babylonian, the

Greek, the Jewish, the Roman, the Hindu, the Chinese, the

Aztec, and the Mayan. In most of these cases, the calendar has
been developed with a certain astronomical knowledge. What
1s the function of this so universal characteristic of culture, or
in other words of social organization?
The most obvious primitive use of astronomy is in navigation.

Nevertheless, calendars are equally important in cultures in
which navigation has played a large role, and in substantially

If, however, we look more carefully at the calendar, we see

that it contains manyfeatures which do not belong to the seasons
and to the solar year. Many cultures have a lunar year, which
is only adjustable by violence to the course of the seasons. The
Moslem year, for example, drifts through its months all around
the solar year, so that no fixed correspondence between the
two years can be established.

There are moreover certain features of the calendar which

only belong in a rough way either to the solar or the lunar,
and which have an importance almost equal to that of these
natural divisions of time. Among these is the week.
Behind the week are at least two social phenomena: religious
worship and the market. The seven-day week is roughly a
quarter of the lunar cycle, and like the lunar cycle, has no
significant relation to the seasons. In China, the ten-day market
period plays a considerable role, and there are still other weeks
in different parts of the world. Why is the week so important
to culture?

Whether we consider the market week or the religious week,
the week is used to regulate certain forms of human activity
which depend on the joint action of large parts of the com-

munity. The religious week regulates the coming together of
people in worship; but it also regulates the rhythm of work and
rest. Without something of this sort, it would be extremely
difficult to bring the community together for a systematic
worship in which large parts of the community take a joint
share. We should furthermore find that the private and separate
chythms of rest and activity of a community interfere sericusly
with the activity of the farmer, the merchant, and the crafts-

man. It is important, in business or political or other communal
activities, to be reasonably sure that a man will be available

for his share in these activities at stated and foreseeable times.

The highest degree of such timed communal activity is to be

found in the market.
Here the farmer knows that at certain stated times, he may
come to the market with his crops, and find somebody ready
to purchase them. The craftsmen knows that he need not
interrupt his work in the shop in an irregular manner, but can
devote a definitely assignable day and even hour to the problem
of the sale of his work. This involves a very different activity

from that of manufacture. Without the market-week, orderly
business and social activity would be greatly hampered.
Besides this sort of rhythm of business and markets, there is
in most cultures a longer rhythm which depends on religious
festivals. Many contracts in Europe are made by the quarter
year, in which such religious feasts as Christmas, Easter,

Whitsuntide and Michaelmas are the critical days.
Certain periods in the year, such as Lent or the fast of
Ramazan among the Moslems, are reserved for the communal
self-abnegation and asceticism, and thus form an essential part
of the mode of life of the community. Let it be noted that Lent
varies over a considerable range with respect to the solar year,

and that Ramazan, like all features of the Moslem calendar,

runs its due course around the seasons. Their clock is artificial,
and not the strict clock of the seasons. It is not too much to say

that all forms of culture depend on the probability that all the
people, or at least a large part of them, will be doing the same
things at the same stated times.

As I have said, this clocking represents a foretaste of the
tighter clocking which takes place in the factory. When a man
punches the time-clock at eight in the morning in order to
assure the employer that he will be available for work together

with the other members of his gang, he is carrying out a process
which is foreshadowed in the market week. When a factory
demands that the raw material be delivered at certain precise

hours of certain precise days, it is only going a little further in
the same direction. The meaning of all these things is nothing
but an awareness of the same factors which demand that the

automatic milling machine contain an internal clock, and of
the further factors which probably create the need for a similar
fine clock in the human brain itself.
